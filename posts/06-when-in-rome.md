Rome is a layer cake of empires, and every corner is a museum nobody bothered to signpost. A week there isn't enough, but it's enough to develop opinions about pasta shapes and strong feelings about Baroque ceiling frescoes.

## The Caravaggio Program

The unofficial theme of the trip was **Caravaggio hunting** — his paintings hang in plain sight in churches across the city, free to view, usually nearly empty:

- **San Luigi dei Francesi** — three St. Matthew paintings in a side chapel, lit by a coin box
- **Sant'Agostino** — the Madonna of the Pilgrims, with the dirty feet that scandalized the church
- **Santa Maria del Popolo** — the Conversion of Paul, all shadow and rump and divine light

Standing alone in front of a Caravaggio in a dark church, dropping coins in the light box like feeding a meter — that's the Roman experience no line at the Colosseum can match.

## The Eating Program

The days had structure: museums and ruins until late afternoon, then the serious business. **Cacio e pepe** in Trastevere. Carbonara judged against an increasingly strict personal rubric. Aperitivo hour observed faithfully. Gelato twice daily, as research.

## The Ruins

The Forum at opening time, before the crowds, when you can hear your own footsteps on two-thousand-year-old stone. The Pantheon's oculus with rain falling through it. Ostia Antica, the old port city, which we had nearly to ourselves — a whole Roman town with no line, no fence, and mosaic floors you can walk on.

> Rome's trick is making the extraordinary feel municipal. A guy sells roasted chestnuts next to a two-thousand-year-old temple and nobody thinks it's weird.

I'll be back. The rubric demands it.
